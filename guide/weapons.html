00 · Why nothing fires

An honest starting point

Before the mechanism, the symptom — because the symptom is misleading, and almost every team misdiagnoses it the same way.

What you will see on day one and what it is not

You run a season with a rack in the hold. Nothing fires. Twenty-seven armed battles, zero salvos. The natural conclusion is the model is not clever enough to use weapons.

It is nothing of the kind. The model is never asked. It is not told it owns a weapon, it is not offered a play that uses one, and the reply format it must answer in physically cannot express one — the JSON schema restricts the verb to four values, and emp_launch is not among them. The model could be a genius and the night would look identical.

The rule that governs this whole guide

Each link is invisible until the one before it works. Write beautiful doctrine on day one and you will observe precisely nothing, because the menu has no weapon play for the doctrine to talk about. Build in order, test after each step, and every step shows you something new.

Two halves, and most teams only find one

There is a firing half — schema, menu, doctrine, packager — and a money half — finding blue, wanting blue, spending blue. The built-in soc weapons ladder only checks the firing half. A fork can pass all four of its rungs and still never fire, because it never bought anything to fire. Both halves are in this guide.

02 · The eleven stages

One tab at a time

Each stage says the same things: what the agent needs to understand, how you know it is missing, what to change — then, side by side, what is in the tree today and what you write. Every snippet is real code.

First, who does what

Worth getting straight before the stages, because the common mental model — “the option is offered, the model picks it, the engine runs it” — skips the step where most of the work happens. Colours follow the agent guide: green is our Python, amber is the model, blue is the engine.

Engine Resolve the night 21 hours, one move per hour, per seat
The consequence, and it is liberating

The model never composes a coordinate. It chooses from a list of pre-computed, pre-validated plays — the menu header tells it so in as many words. So “teach the agent to aim” is barely prompt engineering at all: it is writing the Python that decides which cell is worth hitting, then giving the model an honest reason to pick it.

Which also means a weapon play is three separate pieces of work, not one. Geometry that finds the target (stage 7), a menu entry that offers and prices it (stages 8–9), and a packer that turns the chosen ID back into a launch on the right hour (stage 10). Miss any one and the other two are invisible.

03 · Doctrine, in full

Two kinds, and you need both

Stage 5 says write doctrine; this is what it looks like. There are two kinds and teams routinely write one and wonder why nothing changed. All the text below is real, in the tree, and reaching a model tonight.

1 · Weapon doctrine prose → the prompt

Free text in doctrine.py, gated on holding the weapon. It teaches judgement: what the thing does, when it beats harvesting, at what hour, and — the part people skip — when it is wrong.

Leave out “when it is wrong” and the model fires it every night, including the rich ones where a weapon that banks nothing is a straight loss.

2 · Move doctrine data → the menu

A declared play in weapon_plays.py. It teaches a specific move: this weapon, on this board condition, at this hour, aimed by this resolver, borrowing that play's geometry — plus a why that reaches the menu line.

Weapon doctrine without a declared play argues for a move the menu cannot offer. A declared play without doctrine is a line the model has no framework to judge.

Weapon doctrine — all three, verbatim

Note the shared skeleton: a one-line verdict in caps, the mechanics, the cost, then WHEN IT IS WRONG. Shouted lines are the load-bearing ones, so a skim catches the verdict and misses nothing.

SNAP 100 blue · one cell · one hour the cheapest one to start with 
SNAP — THE CHEAPEST INTERDICTION IN THE GAME.
SPEND A SNAP on the single eye that lights their best target. 100 blue,
one cell, one hour.
It kills a probe on that cell and damages a harvester on it — including
one that ARRIVES during the hour. That last half is what lets a SNAP
GUARD a square rather than merely punish one.
It resolves ABOVE the hour-start vision snapshot, so the landing it
denies is refused TONIGHT rather than delayed. This is the one thing an
EMP cannot do, and it is why SNAP answers a smash-and-grab.
WHEN IT IS WRONG: if they can reach the target from a second eye, or
land from another bearing, you have bought a beat and nothing more.
Count their eyes before you fire.
EMP 200 blue · 3 missiles · 8 hours the one with friendly fire 
EMP — AREA DENIAL YOU CAN STAND IN.
SPEND A CHARGE early or not at all: the cloud runs 8 hours and wants the
night ahead of it, so past hour 2 there is no night left to exploit.
One launch fires up to 3 missiles as ONE move and ONE charge — each
darkens a radius-2 diamond of 13 cells. Probes inside are destroyed;
harvesters inside are disabled hour by hour. A rival cannot drop into
cells it cannot see, so a landing under the cloud is REFUSED, not
delayed.
A cell is only darkened when a missile lands within its radius, and you
choose where they land — so aim at least one cell further out than the
blast reaches and the middle stays clear. That hole is a place only you
can use for the cloud's whole life.
FRIENDLY FIRE IS ON. Your own units in the cloud are not immune.
WHEN IT IS WRONG: darkening ground you wanted to walk. If you have not
planned a hole to stand in, you have denied yourself as well as them.
CHAFF 300 blue · no target · 3 hours the one that costs no credits 
CHAFF — CANCEL THE MOVE, NOT THE PLACE.
SPEND A CHAFF when you hold one and an hour of theirs is worth more than
two of yours.
A chaff is not aimed at a cell. It is aimed at an HOUR: at the hour it
resolves, every OTHER seat's action that hour is cancelled — their drop,
their step, their pickup, their probe. The window runs 3 hours, so there
is no re-try for them inside it.
THE COST IS SYMMETRIC. You are immune only at the hour you launch,
because that slot was spent launching. The next two hours cancel YOUR
moves too. Plan the walk three hours after the flare, never one — a move
ordered inside your own window is simply cancelled, and the night then
reads as a failure that was really a mis-plan.
WHEN IT IS WRONG: if you had a rich walk queued in those hours, you are
paying 300 blue and two hours to deny one. Take the grab instead.

The correction pattern

The subtle one. Your fork already ships a BEWARE_ block per weapon, written from the defender's side. Once the seat is holding the thing, that block is actively misleading — so rather than editing it, land a correction after it. Recency favours whichever came last.

CORRECTION ordered after DOCTRINE_BEWARE_CHAFF fires only when the rack has one 
CORRECTION TO THE CHAFF BLOCK ABOVE — YOU ARE HOLDING ONE.
That block is about SURVIVING a rival's chaff, and it describes chaff as
blind-fired at likely PICKUP hours. That is the defender's view and it
does not describe the weapon in your rack.
Fired deliberately, a chaff cancels a SPECIFIC hour you have read: any
action, not only a pickup. An early flare is not wasted — a drop is an
action, so an hour-1 chaff cancels an hour-1 DROP.
One thing it does not catch (v1.57): a SNAP declared for the very hour
you fire outruns the flare and lands anyway. From the next hour of the
window on it is cancelled like everything else — so jamming a SNAP means
having the window already up, which is a read rather than a reaction.
Do not pre-lose the argument

One reference fork's EMP group header ended “and never over a certain pure/mass grab”, which reads as a rule rather than a trade. The model then refused the weapon three times out of three with the option sitting right there in the prompt.

State the trade honestly in the header and let the per-play why argue the specific case. A header is not the place to settle it.

Move doctrine — one declared play, annotated

And here is the other kind. Five constraints and a sentence; the sentence is what the model actually reads on the menu line.

…/harnesses/<yours>/weapon_plays.py PLAYS 
WeaponPlay(
    play_id="BLIND_THE_FINDER",
    weapon="snap",
    when="redsign_theirs",      # only when a RIVAL lit a pure
    hour="super_early",         # H1 — before their drop lands
    targets="finder_probe",      # the resolver that finds the eye
    min_targets=1,              # no eye found, no option offered
    combines_with="blind_grab",  # borrows that play's geometry
    why=(
        "when a rival has lit a pure we cannot see, the eye that "
        "found it is the only target we can name; snapping it at "
        "hour one refuses their drop for lack of live vision, then "
        "a paired blind-grab combs the smear they can no longer reach"
    ),
)
✓
Read the why again and notice what it is doing. It does not describe the weapon — the weapon doctrine did that. It describes this move on this board, names the follow-up that banks the value, and tells the model the denial is only half the play. That is the sentence that turns a zero-yield option into a ranked one.

The real trade nobody spots credits, not blue

Orbit pays 1000 credits a turn. A probe costs 250. An EMP costs 250 credits on top of its blue, and a SNAP the same. So every unfired charge is a probe you did not build — vision not opened, ground not found, on an agent whose entire edge is knowing where the red is.

This is exactly why V12 stopped buying. Read the comment at the switch; it is the clearest statement of the trade in the repo.

sea_of_colours/orchestrator_2/harnesses/tabula_v12/orbit.py:145
# v14 — V12 does not buy weapons. It never fired one: over nine
# measured seasons the shipped agent bought EMPs and left every
# charge in the rack at settlement, where a charge scores zero.
#
# The cost is not the BLUE, which is also worthless at settlement.
# It is the CREDITS: game/weapons.py prices an EMP at 250 credits
# and a SNAP at 250, and game/session.py prices a PROBE at 250. So
# every unfired charge was a probe not built.
actions, rationale = plan_orbit_actions(agent_view, weapons_enabled=False)
The trap this sets for you

Turning buying back on without closing the firing half makes your agent worse than stock, measurably. You will spend credits on charges you never use and build fewer probes. The two halves have to move together.

What the ladder does not check

It is a smoke test, not a proof, and it is firing-only. It answers “have you built this rung at all”, never “is your implementation any good”. A fork can go green on all four and still fire into empty space. And nothing in it covers stages 1–3 (blue and buying) or stage 11 (feedback) from this guide.

For “is it actually better”, the only honest answer is a comparison: soc suite --agent <yours> --loadout empty,both. If armed and unarmed score the same, it is firing into space.

06 · Silent traps

Four ways to lose an afternoon

These all share a property that makes them expensive: they fail quietly. Nothing throws, nothing logs, the agent just behaves as though you never made the change.

1 · The play whose condition never holds offered never, reported never

A weapon play declares when it applies. If that predicate never matches a real board, the option is simply never built, and nothing anywhere says so. A play called finder_probe shipped dead for exactly this reason: it was in the registry, it borrowed working geometry, and the target mode it declared had never once run.

How to not have this

Validate the declared axes against the allowed sets at build time and raise. An unlisted value that falls through to a default is how a dead play stays invisible for a week.

2 · The wire verb and the frame tag two strings that merely look alike

What you send the engine and what the engine writes into the replay are separate strings. Key your last-night reader on the wrong one and every frame drops out. The agent then journals that its move never executed — and carries that false belief into tomorrow's reasoning.

This happened, and the way it presented is worth knowing: a seat fired a SNAP that fried a rival probe, saw no SNAP in its own log, and wrote “the SNAP denial worked” into its journal anyway. Right by luck — and the guess is what gets carried forward.

3 · The denial play that prices at zero correctly ranked last

The menu ranks options by what they bank. A denial play — a SNAP on a rival's eye, a chaff on their big hour — banks nothing, so against a chain worth 600 it prices at zero and the model sensibly ignores it. It is not being stupid; it is reading the number you gave it.

The fix

Price a denial by what the rival loses, not what you gain, and say so in the menu line. “Blinds the only eye on their pure seam for 8h” is a number the model can rank.

4 · Loosening one blue gate five levers, all pushing the same way

Blue is held back by five independent mechanisms. Loosen one and another still holds, so the observable behaviour does not move at all — which reads exactly like “my edit did nothing”. That is the interesting part of the problem, and it is stage 2.
